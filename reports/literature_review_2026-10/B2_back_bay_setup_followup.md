# B2 — Does open-coast wave setup carry into the back bays, or is back-bay setup made at the inlets?

## 1. The short answer

**Words used here.**
- **Wave setup**: the rise in mean water level that happens where waves break. As waves lose energy in the surf zone, they push the mean water surface up toward the shore.
- **Ebb shoal** (ebb-tidal delta): the sand lobe that sits seaward of a tidal inlet. It is shallow, so big waves break on it.
- **Inlet throat**: the narrowest, deepest section of the inlet channel.
- **Tidal prism**: the volume of water that goes in and out of the bay in one tide.
- **Inlet efficiency** (Keulegan's repletion idea): how easily the inlet lets the bay level follow the ocean level. A wide, deep, short inlet feeding a small bay is "efficient". A long, narrow, shallow one feeding a big bay is not.

**Answer.** Every study that separates the mechanisms says the same thing: the wave setup that raises a back bay is made **at the inlet**. Waves break on the ebb shoal and at the inlet mouth, and that raises the water level right at the mouth. That raised level then drives flow into the bay, and the bay fills up fairly evenly. The setup on the open beach on either side is a different number. Hanslow & Nielsen measured it at the same moment beside a trained entrance (one with jetties): about 1 m on the beach, almost nothing in the entrance. And the size of the inlet setup depends on the inlet's own shape:
- **shallow, narrow or untrained** entrances: about 10–15% of the offshore wave height;
- **deep, wide or trained (jettied, dredged)** entrances: a few percent of the offshore wave height, or "negligible".

The one Sandy decomposition I could read in full (Chincoteague Bay, Beudin et al. 2017) found setup *at the inlets* of 0.55 m and 0.25 m. Those values matched a simple rule that setup at a breaking inlet mouth is about 10% of the inlet depth. Beudin et al. put that inlet setup at 30% of the bay's peak water level.

**What this means for "test 8"** *[inference]*:
- If test 8 imposes a shoreline formula such as Stockdon only along the beach, it will **not** reproduce back-bay setup.
- It can work only if the forcing line crosses each inlet mouth and the value imposed **across the inlet** is an inlet-specific setup, not the beach value. That means a separate number for each inlet, set by its depth, width and jetties.
- Copying the beach Stockdon value into the inlet would probably overstate the bay lift at NJ's jettied, dredged inlets. Stockdon's shoreline setup is several times larger than what the literature reports inside trained entrances.
- No published relation gives that per-inlet number for jettied NJ inlets. The relation that Leijnse et al. (2025) attribute to "Nederhoff et al. 2024b" is still not findable in print.

**How confident can we be?**
- **That bay setup is made at the inlet: moderate to high.** Several independent coupled-model studies and field studies agree: Dodet 2013, Beudin 2017, Reffitt 2020, Wargula 2026 (abstract), Treloar 2011 and Hanslow & Nielsen 1993.
- **The size for NJ's jettied inlets during Sandy: low.** The NJ-specific Sandy decomposition (Defne, Ganju & Moriarty 2019, Barnegat Bay–Little Egg Harbor, with a "no offshore waves" run) was readable only as an abstract. Irish & Cañizares (2009) is still abstract-only, plus secondary accounts.

**What could be established:**

- **Full abstract** (OpenAlex; ABSTRACT ONLY). It is longer than the version quoted in review B:
  > "Offshore wave setup generated by wave breaking during coastal storms can create significant flow through tidal inlets and increase bay flooding. However, these flow contributions are not always considered when determining coastal flooding risk for engineering studies. This paper describes the wave-driven flow process at tidal inlets and demonstrates its impact on bay flooding on Long Island, New York. Numerical simulations using a coupled hydrodynamic and wave model are presented to show contributions to bay water level by wave-driven flow with respect to those by wind surge. These simulations include two major historical coastal storms and a small nor'easter in 2003, during which the model was validated using field measurements of water level. Finally, an approach is introduced for estimating wave-induced flow contributions to bay flooding as a function of inlet and storm characteristics, including tidal prism and wave conditions. The findings of this study indicate that wave-induced flow contributions make up 15–35% of the total storm surge, where the wave-induced flow contribution increases with increased inlet efficiency."
- **Model, storms and absolute numbers** (SECONDARY, via Zhu 2017, University of Delaware MS thesis, ch. 1 p. 1–2; PARTIAL):
  - The Long Island work "computed [nearshore water level, waves and sediment transport] using the software Delft 3D" (Cañizares & Irish 2008).
  - The historical storms were the 1938 hurricane and the 1992 nor'easter.
  - "Irish and Cañizares (2009) also examined the effect of ocean wave setup generated by breaking waves. The bay water level increase was estimated to be 0.1 – 0.2 m for the 1938 and 1992 storms."
- **Site** (SECONDARY, via Beudin et al. 2017 §4): "Similar contribution from wave setup to surge levels was reported in Great South Bay, New-York [Irish and Cañizares, 2009]".
- **Geometry data** (SECONDARY, via Aretxabaleta et al. 2017, Table notes; PARTIAL): Aretxabaleta et al. reuse Irish & Cañizares' Long Island inlet and bay geometry ("previous estimates of the maximum depth and width of the inlet computed from U.S. Army Corps of Engineers condition surveys collected in the late 1990s and the bay areas … [from Irish and Cañizares, 2009]"). That suggests the inlet-efficiency parameter was built from inlet depth, width and bay area *[inference]*.
- **Reference list** (Crossref): it includes Keulegan (1967) "Tidal flow in entrances" and Longuet-Higgins (1963) "A note on wave set-up". That is consistent with a Keulegan-type inlet-efficiency parameter driven by an offshore setup *[inference; the method itself is unread]*.
- **How others cite it** (Semantic Scholar citation contexts; SECONDARY):
  - Texas bridges reports (2010): "the wave setup is estimated at about 15% of the total flood level (Irish and Cañizares, 2009)".
  - Galveston Building-with-Nature study (2015): "a wave-induced set-up of about 15-35% of total storm surge".

**What this does and does not settle:**
- The abstract frames the forcing as **"offshore wave setup"** that drives flow through the inlet, and says the contribution grows with inlet efficiency.
- I could not confirm whether "offshore setup" means open-beach shoreline setup or setup over the ebb shoal and inlet mouth. The simulations were coupled Delft3D, so breaking on the ebb shoal was part of the physics whichever word they used *[inference]*.
- The 15–35% is relative to "total storm surge" in the bay. Absolute values were 0.1–0.2 m for the 1938 and 1992 storms (Zhu 2017).

### Gap 3 — Setup-only nearshore forcing (no wave solver) for back bays

**Status: no peer-reviewed study found that imposes setup only (a mean-level offset, no IG) along a surf-zone line on a barrier coast with inlets and reports back-bay skill.** That includes the SFINCS wavemaker `wstfile`, Delft3D, ADCIRC and SCHISM.

What exists nearby:

- **Leijnse et al. 2025** (in review B): this remains the only explicit comparison of shoreline-formula setup against a coupled wave solver *inside back bays*. Stockdon at the −10 m boundary gave back-bay R² 0.66 and RMSE 0.31 m. SnapWave gave R² 0.75 and RMSE 0.24 m.
- **Engineering practice: imposing an entrance-specific setup at an estuary mouth.** NSW Office of Environment and Heritage (2015), *Floodplain Risk Management Guide: Modelling the Interaction of Catchment Flooding and Oceanic Inundation in Coastal Waterways* (PARTIAL: §2–3, Fig. 5.1 notes, §9, App. A captions). This is a government method that adds setup to the downstream boundary **by entrance type**, not as a single shoreline value:
  - Fig. 5.1 notes: "Group 3 estuaries which have trained entrances and are maintained as navigable ports (e.g. Newcastle Harbour) will have little ocean tide attenuation and negligible wave set-up. These port and harbour estuaries should use Waterway Entrance Type A."
  - "Group 3 estuaries with fully (both sides of entrance) trained entrances which are not maintained as navigable ports will have little ocean tide attenuation but some potential for wave setup and should use Waterway Entrance Type B".
  - Untrained entrances and lagoons (ICOLLs, intermittently closed lakes and lagoons) are Type C.
  - Design boundary series (App. A, 1% AEP, south of Crowdy Head): Type A has no setup term. Type B has "0.55m wave set up". Type C has "1.1m wave setup".
  - §9: "Sensitivity to ocean boundary condition can be tested by increasing the ocean boundary condition … by 0.3 metres to provide an allowance … for wave setup."
  - *[inference]* This is the closest published analogue to test 8 with per-inlet values. It ranks entrances the same way the process studies do: trained and navigable lowest, untrained highest. It is a design rule for a high-energy Australian coast (derived from WRL 2013, not opened), not a hindcast validation.
- **XBeach-transect-forced SFINCS** (Gaido-Lasserre et al. 2024; Storlazzi et al. 2024, USGS DR 1184): reef and island coasts, forced with mean level plus IG. No back-bay or inlet validation (review B).
- **SFINCS v2.4.0 "Galibier" changelog** (2026.01; read this session): "Added option of forcing incident wave energy at wavemaker", "Renaming of wavemaker related input variables (e.g. wavemaker_wvmfile)", and "Added input variable 'snapwave_waveforces_ratio' which you can set to 0 to turn off wave forces and thus incident wave setup". Nothing about a setup-only mode or about inlets.
- **Analytical bay-filling without a wave term** (Zhu 2017; Kobayashi & Zhu 2017, JWPCOE 143(5)), Indian River Inlet, Delaware. This is a jettied navigation inlet *[site knowledge, not from the source]*.
  - A one-inlet analytical model forced only by the Lewes tide gauge (no setup) "predict[s] the peak stillwater elevation within 10% errors at two bay gauges and within 30% errors at the third" for 27 storms in 2005–2015 (thesis ch. 8; PARTIAL).
  - For Sandy, the extra bay rise is attributed to **wave overtopping** of the barrier ("0.1 – 0.2 m in the bay"), not to setup through the inlet.
  - *[inference]* At this deep, jettied inlet, a calibrated no-setup model was enough for most storms. But the calibrated inlet coefficient K* could absorb a mean setup, so this is suggestive, not a test.

### Gap 4 — Sandy-specific decompositions of the wave contribution in back bays

**New in this follow-up (not in review B's bibliography):**

**(a) Beudin, Ganju, Defne & Aretxabaleta 2017, "Physical response of a back-barrier estuary to a post-tropical cyclone", JGR Oceans 122:5888.** Chincoteague Bay (MD/VA), Hurricane Sandy, COAWST (ROMS + SWAN, vortex-force wave coupling). PARTIAL, read from a Wayback copy of the Wiley HTML: abstract, §1, §2.1–2.2, the §4 discussion of the momentum balance and setup, §5.
- Model: "100 m horizontal resolution grid … the bay, the barrier island, both inlets, and approximately 15 km of the inner continental shelf". Scenarios remove swell, local waves and wind one at a time (Table 1).
- Mechanism and magnitude (§4): "At the peak wave height offshore (29 October 16:00), water level change between scenarios S and TSAW (no swell offshore) reached 0.55 and 0.25 m in the north and south inlets, respectively. Wave setup is a response to wave height decrease at the coast …, and the difference of setup between the two inlets reflected the offshore spatial gradients in wave height and bathymetric slope."
- Depth rule (§4): "Assuming that waves break at the mouth of the inlets, their height is proportional to water depth … and therefore, wave setup is limited to ∼10% of water depth at the mouth of the inlets. According to this simple formulation and given that the average depth in Ocean City Inlet and Chincoteague Inlet is 6 and 3 m, wave setup was around 0.6 and 0.3 m, respectively (by comparison the numerical model predicted 0.55 and 0.25 m, respectively)."
- Bay-wide effect (§4): "Remote wave forcing increased water level in the northern portion of the bay by 0.4–0.5 m". Also: "remote wave forcing generated a gradient of wave setup between the two inlets creating a pressure gradient force in the opposite direction to wind stress".
- Share of the bay peak (§5): "The contribution of the wave-induced setup at the inlets to sea level in the bay was 30% of the total peak value."
- Elsewhere: "80% of the water level increase in the bay was due to coastal sea level at the peak of the storm" (abstract), and "Locally generated waves had a negligible effect on water level but reduced the residual flow up to 70%".
- ⚠️ The 80% (coastal sea level) and 30% (inlet setup) figures do not sum to 100%. I did not resolve how they are defined (different reference states or times). Quote each with its own wording.
- Relevance *[inference]*: the setup is set **at the inlet mouths** by inlet depth, then spreads through the bay. A line across each mouth carrying the right level would reproduce the bay lift. A beach value would not.

**(b) Defne, Ganju & Moriarty 2019, "Hydrodynamic and morphologic response of a back-barrier estuary to an extratropical storm", JGR Oceans, doi 10.1029/2019JC015238.** Barnegat Bay–Little Egg Harbor, NJ, Hurricane Sandy, COAWST. ABSTRACT ONLY (Wiley 403, no archived copy).
- Abstract: "explored the role of offshore water levels, offshore waves, local winds, and waves by systematically removing forcings … Offshore water levels had the largest impact on water levels in the bay, while waves and local wind forcing created substantial spatial variation along the longitudinal axis of the bay."
- **The model runs are public.** USGS data release Defne & Ganju 2019, doi 10.5066/P99K85SW (ScienceBase metadata, FULL). It has runs `Storm`, `noSwell`, `noSW`, `noSWW` and `nonStorm` ("we first removed offshore waves (noSwell), then removed locally generated waves (noSW), and finally removed local wind (noSWW)").
- Model resolution "ranged from 40 to 200 m, with the higher resolution located near complex geometry and around the inlets" (Aretxabaleta et al. 2019 §3, describing the same model).
- THREDDS paths: `geoport.usgs.esipfed.org/thredds/catalog/sand/usgs/Projects/BBLEH/run076{,noSwell,noSW,noSWW,nonStorm}/`. The server did not respond on 2026-10-03.
- *[inference]* `Storm` minus `noSwell` at Barnegat and Little Egg inlets and inside the bay would measure the offshore-wave contribution to the Sandy bay peak directly, from an independent model on our own domain. It is the most useful single item this follow-up turned up for the decision.

**(c) Reffitt, Orescanin, Massey, Raubenheimer, Jensen & Elgar 2020, JWPCOE 146 (Nov 2020), "Modeling storm surge in a small tidal two-inlet system".** Katama Bay, Hurricane Irene. ADCIRC+STWAVE at NACCS resolution compared with a high-resolution version. PARTIAL: accepted manuscript via NOAA IR (Wayback), abstract, §2.3.2, §2.4.2, §3.2.2, §4 opening. Also Orescanin et al. 2020 ICCE extended abstract.
- NACCS cannot resolve a small inlet (ICCE abstract): "the grid resolution in the Katama Bay area is on the order of 200 m, insufficient to resolve the small (< 300 m wide) Katama Inlet." In the paper: "The NACCS mesh treats the southern shoreline of Martha's Vineyard … as a hard, no normal-flow boundary".
- Wave coupling adds bay setup (§3.2.2): "KB-CSTORM includes wave-driven setup, and predicts significantly (up to 0.3 m) higher water elevations in the southern part of Katama Bay and in the surf zone directly to the south than are predicted by KB-ADCIRC".
- Also: "during surge events not all wave-driven momentum flux entering the bay through Katama Inlet can be radiated out of the bay through Edgartown Channel, resulting in an overall bay setup that is common in single-inlet systems."
- Ebb-shoal waves: "The KB-CSTORM model predicts ~3 m wave heights on the ebb shoal during the peak of the storm, whereas the NACCS model predicts 1.5 m wave heights".
- Abstract: the high-resolution coupled model "reduced error … by over 30% compared with the lower-resolution NACCS model, and by 16% compared with the high-resolution, uncoupled ADCIRC model."
- Relevance *[inference]*:
  - Inlet-scale setup needs the ebb shoal resolved. NACCS at about 200 m under-forced it here.
  - Review B's ERDC TR-25-18 figure (NACCS-derived back-bay setup of 10–30% of SWL) inherits NACCS's inlet resolution, 70–200 m at NJ inlets. It is not obviously better than our own model at the inlets.

**(d) Wargula, Velasquez-Montoya, Rey & Mulligan 2026, "Bay- and inlet-scale hydrodynamics in a back-barrier system during storms", ECSS 340:109991.** Albemarle–Pamlico Sound and Oregon Inlet. Isaias 2020 and a nor'easter with the remnants of Teddy, not Sandy. ABSTRACT ONLY (ScienceDirect and SSRN blocked).
- "Ocean waves have bay-scale impacts of driving a nearly-uniform increase in water levels throughout the Sound that depends on wave height, direction, and storm duration. Local to the inlet, wave impacts vary spatiotemporally with tidal phase and bathymetry. Currents through the inlet are typically wind-dominated, with wave impacts becoming important only over longer storm durations."
- Relevance: an independent 2026 result that wave forcing raises a large back-barrier sound **nearly uniformly**, through its inlets, and that the effect grows with **storm duration** (the bay has to fill).
