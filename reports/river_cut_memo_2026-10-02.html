Where should v4's rivers end?

v4 runs its rivers far upstream, to where a Sandy-plus-3 m sea would stop. Those reaches are cheap to compute but have cost most of the design time: river walls, mask patches, fake lake beds, and a river bed nobody has surveyed above the Trenton falls. This memo puts four ways of cutting the rivers side by side, plus a fifth that came out of the numbers, so you can pick one.

The short version. Measured against Sandy, today's head of tide costs almost nothing. Above those lines there are 0.27 km² of Sandy's mapped flooding, no high-water marks and no tide gauges, and removing the reaches clears most of v4's open bed problems.

At +3 m the picture splits. Where the head of tide is a dam or falls (Trenton, Philadelphia, Dundee, Oradell, Bound Brook, Rahway, the Christina), the +3 m run raises the water above the line by only 0–0.11 m, so the dam holds. Where it is a soft tidal limit (the Cohansey at Bridgeton, the Neshaminy), the full sea-level rise goes straight past it.

Measured since (3 October): A and A+ are built and run. They score exactly like v4 at every mark, and below every cut the peak water matches v4's to about 1 cm, even at +3 m on the Cohansey and Neshaminy. Walling a soft river doesn't back the sea up below the wall. So A+ differs from A only in the +3 m flooding it keeps above Bridgeton and Langhorne.

Whole watersheds (D) are a different model: 1.3–1.6 times the ring's area again, in new land. No published group has used a rule like the current +3 m one (C).

The Maurice result can't be trusted yet. v4 has Union Lake as a pit near sea level (CUDEM's flat fill), so the model's "lake" fills from the tide at +0 m already (peak 0.75 m against a real surface near 7.6 m). Whether +3 m would really overtop the dam depends on a crest height nobody has checked.

Measured on A (3 October): moving the Maurice inflow down to the dam raises the river below Millville by 1.2 m in the Sandy run. That exposes another bed fault. In v4's Sandy run, the Maurice 3–10 km below Millville is never wet at all, although the tide stands at 1.25 m further down. Something in the bed blocks it (not yet located). No marks or gauges are there.

Not removed by any cut: the other 89 small lakes on the parked list (5.7 km²). They sit on the coastal plain, not on the river reaches, and the creek channels missing at four Delaware-tributary gauges are all downstream of these lines.

The marks can't tell the three apart, as expected: none of the 166 marks or gauges sit in the trimmed reaches. Run times are the same within node-to-node noise (1.6–2.2 h for all three).

The water does rise against the new walls at +3 m (2.9 m at the Hackensack, 2.4 at the Cohansey, 1.3 at Bound Brook). That's the sea rising against the end of the model, as it would at any tidal cut. Whether it would have gone past the line is the river-by-river table above, read from v4. The two places where A's peak differs from v4's by more than a few centimetres are bed faults the cut only exposes: the Schuylkill (+0.1 m) and the Maurice (+1.2 m in the Sandy run, +0.1 m at +2 and +3 m).

Whole watersheds (D)

Extending v4 to the divides adds 37,500 km² of land outside the ring, about 1.6 times the ring itself. Stopping below the big reservoirs, the way the North Carolina group did, takes off about 6,800 km² (the three NYC reservoirs are a third of that) and leaves 30,800 km².

Runtimes assume cost grows with cells and the run window grows about 2.5× for river spin-up and base flow (Trenton was already at 390 m³/s on 26 October). River corridors at 25 m add another 0.6–0.95 M cells.

Compute is the smaller cost. The data are the hard part: rain and infiltration calibrated over the Pennsylvania and New York uplands, reservoir releases (no 2012 record for Wallenpaupack or the Mongaup chain), and channel beds for 2,500–3,000 km of river. That is the same unsurveyed-bed problem v4 already has, a hundred times over. D earns its keep in rain-driven storms. Sandy in New Jersey wasn't one: USGS reports that "widespread river flooding was not documented".

Cutting at each gauge (B)

Of the 61 gauges, 39 sit inside the ring, so the cut would move downstream to the gauge (median 1.6 km, up to 12.2 km), and 22 sit outside, so the domain would grow upstream to reach them (median 2.9 km, up to 16.7 km). The cell count barely changes.

The catch is that 26 of the 39 inside gauges sit in the +3 m target, including the two biggest inflows (Trenton and Philadelphia). B gives up the +3 m rule on those rivers.

In return, the drainage-area scaling questions go away, because each river enters exactly where it is measured. That holds only where the land between gauge and edge comes into the model. On five "partial" rivers that is a small version of D: Great Egg 251 km², Maurice 176, Middle Brook 40, Mispillion 21, Mantua 16.

On the big rivers, B and A mostly agree: the Raritan, Passaic, Hackensack and Brandywine gauges sit within 2 km of their head-of-tide lines.

What the literature says

  • The head of tide is the usual cut for estuary surge studies, including on our neighbours: the Delaware to Trenton, the Hudson to the Troy dam, and a New Jersey Sandy study that stopped the Passaic at Dundee Dam and the Hackensack below Oradell.
  • No published study draws its domain from a future water level. Sea-level-rise studies reuse their present-day domain. The closest "SLR-ready" practice is a fixed contour (+10 m NAVD88; FEMA Region II uses 25 ft) with rivers entering where they cross it.
  • Models that reach far inland score much worse inland: about 0.1 m error at the coast against 0.6–0.7 m at inland marks (Nederhoff, Ye). The authors blame unsurveyed channel beds and errors in river flow and rain.
  • Where the river cut sits has never been tested directly. Only "river on vs off" exists. That moved coastal gauges by a few centimetres (about 2 % at the Battery in Irene) and grows upriver.
  • Whether the head of tide moves with sea-level rise: the literature expects soft tidal limits to creep upstream and dams or bedrock not to, but nobody has measured it. Our +3 m run is now a measurement of exactly that, on this estuary (the table above).
  • A caution for any +3 m model: on the Delaware at +1 m, walling the low land instead of letting it flood raised the upper-estuary tidal range by up to 25 cm (Lee et al. 2017). How a +3 m model is closed off changes its water levels.

What a choice would rewrite

Two standing rules decide this, and any option other than C rewrites at least one of them on purpose:

  • "Do not exclude upriver communities Sandy flooded." A and A+ barely touch it for Sandy: 0.27 km² of mapped flooding, at Bridgeton. On the Raritan, the line used here is at Bound Brook, where Sandy's marks end. The true head of tide is lower, at New Brunswick, and cutting there would drop roughly 2.4 km² of mapped flooding and at least one mark (a rough count, not yet re-measured).
  • "The ring contains the Sandy +3 m water." A breaks it on the Cohansey and Neshaminy, A+ keeps it everywhere tested, and B breaks it on 26 rivers. If +3 m stops being a requirement, A is the straightforward answer.

How I read it (the decision is yours).

If v4 only needs to hindcast Sandy, cut at the head of tide (A). It gives up nothing the observations can see and closes most of the open bed work.

If +3 m stays a requirement, A+ looks like the best trade. Cut at the dams and falls, where the +3 m run shows the line holds. Keep the existing reaches on the Cohansey and the Neshaminy, where it doesn't. Decide the Maurice once Union Lake's bed and dam crest are known.

The runs make the A-vs-A+ choice narrower than it looked: A+ protects nothing downstream. It's purely whether you want +3 m flood maps above Bridgeton and Langhorne. Either way, two bed faults stay to fix: the Schuylkill below the Fairmount Dam and the blocked Maurice below Millville.

D is a different project; I would only take it on if rain-driven storms join the brief. B solves a scaling problem that A mostly removes anyway.

On test 8, the review found no case where setup imposed at the shoreline carried into the back bays. What it did find: setup added at the offshore boundary lifts everything, coast and bays alike, and only setup generated inside the model reaches the bays without lifting the coast. NACCS already carries some wave setup into the NJ back bays. Nothing so far makes test 8 look like a sure bet.

Settled since (3 October). Our own runs show where the bays' wave lift is made. On the line where test 8 would impose setup (about 5 m deep), the full model lifts the water by only 0.035 m. The bay gauges lift 2–8× that (0.06–0.22 m), and they keep 75–100 % of it even with wind-grown waves switched off. So the lift is ocean swell breaking at and around the inlet mouths, carried into the bays. A follow-up literature search says the same: back-bay setup is made at the inlet, and its size depends on the inlet (a few percent of the offshore wave height at jettied entrances like New Jersey's). A shoreline formula along the beach would overstate it. Making test 8 work would mean choosing a value for each inlet by hand. That's calibration, not physics, and hourly SnapWave already keeps the whole gain at half the cost.

How I read the wave half (3 October; the decision is yours).

The Cape May pocket comes from the 10° direction bins. With hourly updates alone, the 1,572 faces in the jetty's lee match the full model (−1 cm), against about +1 m in both 10° runs.

The clean cheap setting is hourly updates only: 0.53× the full model's solve time, the marks tie it, and it keeps the whole waves-on gain. The one visible change is the NY bays, about 4 cm higher (closer to the marks, same error). That's a sign that peak water there depends on the wave timing, which ties into the open question about waves making the NY bays slosh.

Adding the 10° bins cuts another third off (0.36×) and still ties at the marks, but it brings the Cape May pocket. That's acceptable only with the pocket flagged or fixed.
